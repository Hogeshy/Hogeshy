Hogeshy
Was Looking
for Something

A story in forty days.

About a black cat, a room, and the part of the self that learns to look back without judging.

Author's note

This story grew from the first forty Hogeshy images, the daily records, and the feelings that gave Hogeshy its shape.

Hogeshy was born during a period of leave from work due to adjustment disorder. It began with looseness and emptiness, became a black cat through a love of cats, and kept one small wish: to be happy without forcing anything.

The story is surreal, but its emotional center is real. The can, the box, the key, the light, and the eye on the tail are not random clues. They are ways of giving shape to feelings that are difficult to explain directly.

Read the novel first. The notes and links at the end explain the connections.

PROLOGUE

Prologue — The Room

There was a room that did not belong to any particular morning.

The curtains were always the color of rain. The clock worked when it wanted to. The window showed a street, although Hogeshy could never remember having seen anyone enter or leave it. On the floor sat an empty cat-food can, a little dented on one side.

Hogeshy lived there because there was nowhere else to go, and because, for a while, going anywhere else seemed like an unnecessarily complicated idea.

No one had told Hogeshy to stay. No one had told Hogeshy to leave. This was important. A command, even a gentle one, could become heavy when the body had spent too long carrying things it could not name.

So Hogeshy remained.

Some days were just another day. Some days had cat food and a strange sense of peace. Some nights the city stayed awake while Hogeshy did not. The room asked for nothing. It did not congratulate Hogeshy for getting out of bed. It did not complain when Hogeshy went back to it.

There was, however, one unusual thing about Hogeshy. At the end of its tail was an eye.

The eye did not blink often. It looked behind Hogeshy when Hogeshy looked ahead. It watched the room when Hogeshy watched the window. It was not an eye for seeing farther. It was an eye for seeing back.

Hogeshy had made it during a difficult period of leave from work, although Hogeshy did not think of it as making anything. The black shape had simply gathered itself from exhaustion, emptiness, a love of cats, and the stubborn wish to be happy without having to perform happiness.

Hogeshy did not know that this wish would become a story. At first it was only a body on a floor, a tail turning once, and a thought that arrived without being invited: Something is missing.

PART I

Part I — Nothing Special

On the first day, nothing happened. This was not entirely true, because Hogeshy woke up, looked at the ceiling, ate, and watched the light move across the wall. But none of these things seemed important enough to report.

Just another day, Hogeshy thought. The sentence fell into the room and stayed there.

On the second day, Hogeshy opened a can. The sound was small and metallic. It travelled farther than expected, struck the window, and came back softer. Hogeshy ate slowly. There was only Hogeshy, cat food, and a strange sense of peace. The peace did not announce itself. It sat beside the empty can.

I think that is enough, Hogeshy thought. The tail-eye looked at Hogeshy as if checking the measurement.

At night, the city remained awake. Its windows flickered, its buses passed, and somewhere a person laughed too loudly. Hogeshy watched the void for a while. Maybe tomorrow would be different. Probably not. The thought was not hopeful or hopeless. It was simply available.

On the fourth day, there was nothing left in the can. Hogeshy turned it upside down. Nothing fell out. It was enough anyway.

The next morning, the window was looking back.

Hogeshy stared at the glass. In it was a black cat with cream-colored eyes, a tail with another eye, and a room behind it. The reflection seemed accurate, which made it suspicious. Hogeshy lifted one paw. The reflection lifted its paw. Hogeshy lowered it. The reflection lowered it too.

The tail-eye did not look at the reflection. It looked over Hogeshy's shoulder.

Dinner came and went. Hogeshy waited after it was over. Waiting had become a shape in the room. It sat opposite Hogeshy like a quiet guest who had forgotten why it came.

On the seventh day, something appeared near the chair. It was not large. It was not wrapped. It might have been a folded piece of paper, or a scrap of light that had fallen out of the window. Hogeshy touched it with one claw.

You can keep this one, said a voice.

Hogeshy looked around. The room was empty. The tail-eye looked behind.

Almost asleep, Hogeshy closed its eyes. The tail disagreed. It remained open, watching the dark beyond the dark.

On the ninth day, the people outside were still awake. Not my problem, Hogeshy thought. But Hogeshy continued looking until the lights became small and the window became black.

On the tenth day, Hogeshy searched the room without standing up. Under the chair: dust. Beside the can: a thread. Behind the curtain: the same street. Nothing had moved, except the feeling that something should have been there.

Something is missing, Hogeshy thought.

The tail-eye looked directly at Hogeshy for the first time.

日記 INTERLUDE I

Diary Interlude I

The diary did not say that Hogeshy was sad. It said that the sky was heavy, that a finger had been pressed against glass, that a sound came from inside the wall. It said the calendar showed yesterday's date again.

This was how the diary worked. Feelings did not arrive as explanations. They arrived as weather, furniture, clocks, and small things that were almost nothing. The record kept them without forcing them to become clear.

PART II

Part II — The Outside

The next night ended, but Hogeshy remained. Morning placed a pale rectangle on the floor. It did not ask whether Hogeshy was ready.

Hogeshy opened the door and went outside for one minute.

The outside had lights. It had wet pavement, wires, distant voices, and a smell that did not belong to the room. Hogeshy stood beneath the building and counted three windows before returning. It was not a journey. It was a minute.

Something was awake somewhere beyond the street. Hogeshy pretended not to notice.

It was not important, Hogeshy told the tail-eye. The eye did not answer.

On the sixteenth day, someone tried to reach Hogeshy. The phone made a sound. The sound travelled through the room and became a shape beside the empty chair. Hogeshy picked up the phone, read the message, and placed it face down.

The message said: Are you there?

Hogeshy thought about writing back. I think, Hogeshy wrote. Then Hogeshy deleted it. It was not a lie. It was not yet a reply.

Rain began doing something outside. It ran down the window in lines that crossed and separated. Hogeshy watched until the room was reflected in the glass. For a moment, the reflection seemed to be standing farther away.

On the nineteenth day, another sound came from the door. Hogeshy did not open it.

There were many reasons. The room was warm. The outside was wet. The body was tired. The person on the other side might want an answer. None of these reasons was large enough to be called a decision, but together they held the door closed.

On the twentieth day, nothing was solved. The message remained unanswered. The door remained closed. The missing thing remained missing.

Hogeshy ate the last of the cat food and sat beside the window.

It was enough.

日記 INTERLUDE II

Diary Interlude II

The diary wrote: The shadow did not match the body. It wrote: Nobody noticed. It wrote: Nothing changed.

These were not contradictions. A thing could be wrong and remain unchanged. A person could be noticed by no one and still be there. The diary was learning how to leave space around a fact.

PART III

Part III — The Envelope

On the twenty-first day, there was an envelope on the floor.

It had no name. It had no stamp. It had no reason to be there. Hogeshy circled it twice. The tail-eye watched from behind.

Hogeshy did not open it.

By the next morning, the floor was wet. Rain had not come inside. The envelope was dry. A line of footprints began at the door and ended beside it. They were too small to belong to a person and too straight to belong to a cat.

At the end of the footprints lay a key.

Hogeshy smelled it. It smelled like somewhere else.

The key was cold. When Hogeshy held it, the room became slightly larger. The ceiling moved away. The door seemed to wait.

On the twenty-fifth day, the key was gone. Beneath the door, light appeared. It was not morning light. It was a thin, blue-white line, as if another room were awake on the other side.

Hogeshy pressed one paw against the door. The light vanished.

The box arrived without arriving. It was simply there beside the chair, damp on the inside and empty on the outside. Hogeshy lifted the lid. The smell of rain came out.

Under the box was a note. The writing disappeared before Hogeshy finished reading it.

There had been only one sentence: You already know where this opens.

Hogeshy looked at the key, though the key was no longer there.

The shadow moved a little later than Hogeshy. When Hogeshy turned, the shadow finished turning after a pause. Hogeshy did not mention it. A shadow was allowed to be tired.

Nothing came into the room the next day. Something was already outside.

Hogeshy stood at the window until the glass became a second room. In that room, everything was in the right place.

That was the problem.

日記 INTERLUDE III

Diary Interlude III

A sound came from inside the wall. The tail moved once. There was a smell of rain indoors. Nothing changed.

The diary did not explain the sound because the diary understood that naming a thing did not always make it safer. It kept the sentence. It left the rest alone.

PART IV

Part IV — The Room Again

On the thirty-first day, the room was the same. The furniture was not.

The chair stood where the window should have been. The table had become narrow. The door was on the wrong wall. Hogeshy sat down anyway.

The next day, the can was not there. Hogeshy looked behind itself. It was.

This happened three times. Each time, the can was closer to the place where Hogeshy had been looking before looking somewhere else.

Hogeshy began to understand. The room was not moving the objects. The room was following attention.

On the thirty-third day, Hogeshy watched the outside. The reflection did not. It looked somewhere else, toward the dark corner behind the room.

Hogeshy turned. Nothing was there except the tail-eye, looking back.

The key returned on the thirty-fourth day. There was no keyhole. Hogeshy kept it anyway.

The box was open on the next day. Inside it was another box. Inside that box was a folded piece of paper. Inside the paper was no message at all.

Hogeshy left everything open.

The shadow stayed behind when Hogeshy walked across the room. It was probably tired. Hogeshy waited for it. The shadow did not move.

On the thirty-seventh day, the blank note showed Hogeshy its other side. Not a written side. A watching side. Hogeshy saw itself there: black, small, tired, and still alive.

There was a light outside. Hogeshy moved closer. The light moved away.

Hogeshy moved again. The light moved again.

The tail-eye was not looking at the light. It was looking at Hogeshy.

And then Hogeshy understood what the eye had been doing all along.

It had not been watching for danger. It had been watching for effort. It had been measuring the moment when looking became forcing, when waiting became punishment, when the wish to be happy turned into another task to complete.

The eye had been Hogeshy's distance from itself. The part that could say: You are here. You are tired. You do not have to become someone else before you are allowed to stay.

On the fortieth day, the can was there. The box was there. The key was there.

Hogeshy had been looking for something. The objects had been telling the truth, but not the truth Hogeshy expected. The can meant the body had been fed. The box meant emptiness could be held without being filled. The key meant a door could exist without being opened. The light meant that outside was real, not that Hogeshy had to go there.

The envelope had come from the only place it could have come from: the room itself, carrying the record of all the things Hogeshy had survived without calling them survival.

The note had disappeared because it had never been a message from someone else. It had been a sentence Hogeshy was not ready to read.

The delayed shadow was the past: always arriving a moment after the body, never quite able to catch up. The reflection was the future: moving in another direction, not as a promise but as a possibility.

Hogeshy looked at the key. Then at the box. Then at the can.

Nothing in the room needed to be solved.

Hogeshy stopped looking.

EPILOGUE

Epilogue — A Small Place to Stop

The room became the room again.

The chair was by the window. The table was where it had always been. The empty can rested on the floor, dented on one side. The box was open. The key lay inside it, although there was still no keyhole anywhere.

Outside, the city continued. Someone was awake. Someone was waiting. Someone might try to reach Hogeshy again.

Hogeshy did not promise to answer. Hogeshy did not promise never to answer. The future could keep its own shape.

A small patch of sunlight reached the floor.

Hogeshy sat down.

The tail-eye looked back, not to judge, but to notice.

Hogeshy was still not entirely sure what happiness was. It might have been a warm floor. It might have been cat food. It might have been the absence of a demand.

For now, that was enough.

Diary interludes

Why the diary is written this way

Hogeshy's diary rarely says, “I am sad,” or, “I am recovering.” Instead it records weather, walls, windows, dates, shadows, and sounds that may not have happened. This is intentional.

When a person is exhausted or empty, feeling does not always arrive as a clear explanation. It may arrive as a flat sky, a clock skipping a beat, a finger against glass, or the sense that a shadow belongs to someone else. The diary gives those sensations a place to exist without forcing them to become an answer.

The diary is also the space between the Days. The Day images show what can be seen. The diary keeps what Hogeshy could not yet say.

Reading notes

How the mysteries connect

The eye on the tail

The eye represents distance from the self. Hogeshy does not use it to judge or predict. It notices when looking has become forcing. In the ending, the eye looks back because Hogeshy finally sees its own exhaustion with gentleness.

The envelope and the disappearing note

The envelope comes from the room itself: from the record of days Hogeshy survived without calling them survival. The disappearing note is not a message from a stranger. It is a sentence Hogeshy has not yet been able to read: that staying, resting, and being unfinished are also forms of living.

The key and the door

The key stands for the possibility of leaving, not an order to leave. There is no keyhole because the story is not about escaping the room perfectly. The key can be kept until Hogeshy is ready, or kept without ever being used.

The box

The box is a container for emptiness. Its inside is wet because the outside world has reached the room as a trace. When another box appears inside it, the search threatens to become endless. Hogeshy leaves it open instead of turning every mystery into another task.

The delayed shadow and the different reflection

The delayed shadow is the past: it follows, but never at exactly the same speed. The reflection looking elsewhere is the future: not a promise and not a command, only another possible direction. Neither one needs to be defeated.

Why Hogeshy stops looking

Hogeshy does not stop because nothing matters. Hogeshy stops because the search has become another demand. The can, box, and key reveal that the things being sought were already present in different forms: nourishment, a place to hold emptiness, and the possibility of a door. The final choice is to remain without forcing a conclusion.
